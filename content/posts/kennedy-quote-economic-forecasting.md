This is the first post in a new weekly feature — **The Kennedy Weekly**. Each post takes one quote from Louisiana's senior senator, **John Kennedy**, and treats it the way good political writing should: not as a sound bite to share but as a starting point for unpacking what the senator is actually saying and what's true, half-true, or wrong about the claim.

Sen. Kennedy is widely known for folksy one-liners that go viral on social media. Those one-liners are usually compressed versions of substantive critiques he's developed in years on the Senate Judiciary Committee, Banking Committee, and Appropriations Committee. The point of this series is to slow down and look at the substance.

For the inaugural post, I picked a quote with real policy weight rather than maximum viral reach.

## The quote

> "You can find an analyst to say just about anything you want to say... There are 300 Ph.Ds and economists at the Fed, and not a single one of them called a meltdown in '08... Economic forecasting around this place in the last 10 years — makes those psychic hotlines look reputable."
>
> — Sen. John Kennedy (R-LA)

It's classic Kennedy: a Southern simile doing the work of a paragraph of policy critique. The line is funny. The substance underneath it is serious.

## What he's actually claiming

Three distinct claims are stacked into the quote:

1. **You can find an analyst to support almost any policy position.** True, trivially. Pick a topic and you'll find a credentialed economist on each side.
2. **The Federal Reserve has roughly 300 PhD economists on staff and missed the 2008 crisis.** Approximately true. The Fed and Federal Reserve Banks together employ hundreds of PhD economists. None of them publicly called the timing or severity of the 2008 financial collapse.
3. **Economic forecasting at the Fed has been worse than chance over a sustained period.** This is the strongest claim and the one worth examining seriously.

## Was the Fed actually blind to 2008?

The short answer is: yes, the Federal Reserve's official forecasts and public statements through 2007 and into 2008 did not anticipate the scale of the crisis that was about to unfold.

Documented examples that have entered the public record:

- **June 2007:** Then-Chairman Ben Bernanke testified before the Joint Economic Committee that subprime lending problems were "likely to be contained."
- **January 2008:** Fed staff forecasts presented at the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) projected continued positive GDP growth for 2008. The recession had technically already started in December 2007.
- **The FOMC transcripts from 2008** — released years later, as is standard practice — show committee members through mid-2008 still treating inflation as a comparable concern to recession risk, with the magnitude of the financial system stress not fully grasped until September.

The post-mortem analyses are not from Kennedy partisans. **The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's own [working papers](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports.html) and after-action reviews** acknowledged the forecasting miss. The **[Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's 2011 report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf)** documented at length how the Fed underestimated counterparty risk in the shadow banking system.

So claim (2) lands. The institution missed it, and the institution has, to its credit, said so.

## Is forecasting at the Fed worse than chance?

This is where Kennedy's quip overstates the case — but not by as much as you'd think.

### What the research actually shows

The Federal Reserve's own studies of its forecasting record (commonly called the Greenbook forecasts, now SEP — Summary of Economic Projections) show:

- **Short-horizon forecasts (one to two quarters out)** are reasonably accurate, beating naive baselines (e.g., "next quarter will look like this quarter") by meaningful margins.
- **Medium-horizon forecasts (4-8 quarters out)** are roughly comparable to private-sector forecasts, neither markedly better nor worse.
- **Forecasts of turning points** — recessions starting, recoveries beginning, inflation regimes changing — are systematically poor. The Fed and most professional forecasters fail to predict these in advance.

This pattern is not unique to the Fed. **Daniel Kahneman, Nassim Taleb, and Philip Tetlock** have each written at length about the limits of expert prediction in domains with complex feedback loops, nonlinearity, and rare-but-severe events. Tetlock's two-decade study of expert political forecasters concluded that the average expert was "roughly as accurate as a dart-throwing chimpanzee" on questions involving more than 3-5 year horizons.

So a fair version of Kennedy's claim is: **the Fed's forecasts are about as good as anyone's at short horizons, and dramatically worse than they're treated as being at the horizons that matter most for policy.** That's a serious critique. It's also, broadly, the academic consensus among honest practitioners of macroeconomic forecasting.

### Where the quip overreaches

The "psychic hotlines" line is rhetorical hyperbole. The Fed's nowcasts and one-quarter-ahead forecasts genuinely add information. Pretending they're worthless misrepresents the institution.

## Why this critique is bipartisan

What makes this quote worth sitting with is that the substance isn't partisan. The same critique has been made by:

- **Larry Summers** (former Treasury Secretary under Clinton, NEC Director under Obama) — repeatedly, on the Fed's pattern of underestimating inflation persistence and overestimating its own forecasting accuracy.
- **Nassim Nicholas Taleb** — for two decades, on the broader failure of mainstream economics to model fat-tailed risks.
- **Raghuram Rajan** (former IMF Chief Economist, former Reserve Bank of India Governor) — famously warned of the 2008 crisis in 2005, was dismissed at the time, and has since written extensively on institutional groupthink in central banking.
- **Sheila Bair** (former FDIC Chair) — on the Fed and Treasury's pre-crisis underestimation of systemic risk.

When a Republican from Louisiana and a Democratic ex-Treasury Secretary land on similar critiques of central bank forecasting, the critique is not really about partisanship. It's about the limits of expertise in complex adaptive systems.

## The policy stakes

This is where the deep dive matters. The credibility of Fed forecasts is not an academic question — it drives real decisions:

- **Interest rate policy** is set in part on the basis of forecasts about inflation and growth.
- **Bank regulatory stress tests** use forecast scenarios to evaluate capital adequacy.
- **Fiscal policy** at Congress and Treasury is calibrated partly to the Fed's outlook.
- **Financial markets** price assets partly on the assumption that the Fed is a competent forward-looking actor.

If the institution's forecasts are systematically miscalibrated at longer horizons — and the evidence suggests they are — every policy decision built on those forecasts inherits the miscalibration. That has direct effects on jobs, mortgages, retirement portfolios, and the cost of living for ordinary Americans.

Sen. Kennedy's framing is folksy. The implication is consequential.

## What he might be missing

A fair-minded counterpoint:

- The Fed publishes uncertainty bands around its forecasts. The "psychic hotline" framing implicitly compares the central estimate to a hard prediction, when the institution itself acknowledges (in its formal documents, if not its public statements) significant uncertainty.
- Coordinating monetary policy without *any* forward look is not a workable alternative. The choice is not between forecasting and not forecasting; it's between humble, uncertainty-aware forecasting and overconfident point estimates.
- Some of the Fed's worst forecasting moments have been when external political pressure has compressed its options, not when it was operating purely on internal expert judgment.

A thoughtful Kennedy critique should land on "the Fed should be more humble about its forecasts, more transparent about its uncertainty, and more cautious about treating its outlook as fact" — which is a defensible position. The viral version that lands on "Fed economists are worthless" is shorter and gets more retweets but misses the actual fix.

## What I take from this

Three things:

1. **The folksy framing is a feature, not a bug.** When a Senator can compress a serious critique of central bank credibility into one Louisiana simile, more voters will actually engage with the substance than if he gave a 40-minute floor speech. The form is doing real work.
2. **The substance, taken seriously, points toward institutional reform** — better uncertainty quantification at the Fed, more humility in forward guidance, more honest acknowledgment of forecasting limits at long horizons. None of this is radical.
3. **The reflexive dismissal of populist critiques of expert institutions** is its own intellectual failure. When the line about psychic hotlines lands as well as it does, it's because the underlying claim — that experts are sometimes treated as more authoritative than their track record warrants — is not wrong.
